The last section in the chapter has looks at proving claims in roundabout ways, rather than directly going from the given to the conclusion.

## Proof by Contradiction

A **contradiction** is when you have two statements that both can't be true. Saying that today is Thursday while also saying today is Friday is a contradiction. Both can't possibly be true.

A **proof by contradiction** starts with assuming what you want to prove is false. For example, "Alani walked 8 kilometers the past two days, so she must have walked at least 4 kilometers on one of those days."

Since the statement is claiming that Alani walked at least 4 kilometers on one of those days, let's assume she _didn't_ do that. We'll instead claim she walked less than 4 on both days.

Now we have a contradiction. If she walked 8 kilometers over two days, there's no way to add two numbers and get 8 without one of them being at least 4. So, the original claim must be true.

For another example, we can prove that there is no "biggest number" by finding a contradiction. If we assume the opposite, that there is some number out there that bigger than all the others, we could easily add 1 to it to get a bigger number. So, we can't have both a "biggest number" and the ability to add one to it, so there must not be a biggest number.

## Proof by Contrapositive

Another way to indirectly prove something is to use its contrapositive. For any conditional to be true, its contrapositive must also be true. So, $\p\to q$ and $\neg q \to \neg p$ are both valid statements. For example, "if it's raining, then I will bring my umbrella" and "if I didn't bring my umbrella, then it is not raining".

Depending on the conditional, the contrapositive might be easier to prove than the original.
